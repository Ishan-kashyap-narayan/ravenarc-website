The cost of the decision you haven’t made

Postponed choices are the largest unmeasured line item on most balance sheets.

By RavenArc · 26 September 2026 · 2 min read

Every leadership team keeps a list it rarely writes down. The underperforming division everyone agrees should be sold, but not this year. The pricing change that would lift margin, but might unsettle a large customer. The plant that should close, the product line that should be retired, the reorganisation that keeps slipping into the next planning cycle.

None of these choices shows up in the accounts. That is precisely the problem. A decision that is made gets a business case, a budget and a review. A decision that is postponed gets nothing, even though it is costing the business money every quarter it waits.

Why good leaders postpone

Postponement is rarely laziness. It is usually a rational response to uncertainty. When the information is incomplete and the downside is visible, waiting feels prudent. Each quarter brings a little more data, and the case for acting always seems one analysis away from being settled.

There are also quieter forces at work. The costs of acting are concentrated and personal: a difficult conversation, a write-down, a reorganisation that falls on named colleagues. The costs of waiting are diffuse and shared. No one is blamed for a margin that erodes slowly, or for capital that stays tied up in a business that no longer earns its keep.

The three costs of waiting

The first is the running cost. Capital, management time and talent remain committed to a position the organisation already doubts. The second is the option cost. Markets move, buyers change their minds and windows close, so the best version of the decision is often available only for a limited time. The third is the signalling cost. When a known problem goes unaddressed, the organisation learns that difficult choices are negotiable, and discipline weakens everywhere else.

These costs compound. A business that postpones one hard decision a year soon carries a portfolio of them, each one quietly diluting returns.

Making the invisible visible

The remedy starts with measurement. Ask the leadership team to name the decisions they have deferred for more than two planning cycles, and then to estimate, even roughly, what each month of waiting costs. The numbers are rarely precise, but they are almost always larger than anyone expected, and they change the conversation from whether to act to how soon.

The second step is to be explicit about what would change the answer. Most postponed decisions are waiting for evidence that is never formally requested. Naming it, and setting a date by which it must arrive, turns an open-ended delay into a bounded one.

The third step is to separate the decision from its execution. Leaders often defer a choice because the implementation looks daunting. Deciding the direction now, and sequencing the execution deliberately, lets the organisation stop paying the cost of waiting while it prepares to act well.

The most expensive decision on the agenda is often the one that is not on it at all.

A question for your next leadership meeting

Ask the team which decision the business would make today if it were starting from scratch, and why it has not made it yet. The answers will tell you where the next gains in margin and growth are hiding.

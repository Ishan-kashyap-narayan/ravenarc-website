The chemist is the sentinel

India’s neighbourhood pharmacies see illness before its hospitals do, and the sales data to prove it already exists. It is built to sell medicines. Used with care, it could tell governments, NGOs and funders where an outbreak is starting, weeks before the official count catches up.

By Lucienne Okafor, Fellow, Growth & Marketing Analytics · 13 October 2026 · 11 min read

In the spring of 1993, people in Milwaukee started buying antidiarrhoeal medicine, and pharmacies sold out early. Nobody yet knew why. The cause turned out to be Cryptosporidium in the city’s water supply. An estimated 403,000 people fell ill, in what remains the largest waterborne outbreak documented in the United States. The water was not recognised as the cause until at least two weeks after people began falling sick. Looking back, public health officials concluded that the rise in medicine sales had been one of the earliest signs. The shop counters knew before the system did.

India ran its own version of this experiment in 2020, with less finesse. As COVID-19 spread, several states told chemists to record everyone who bought medicine for fever, cough or cold. In Karnataka, about 13,000 retail chemists sent buyers’ names, addresses and phone numbers to the health department. In July 2020 the state revoked the licences of 110 pharmacies that had not sent in their sales data, 70 of them in Kalaburagi alone.

The instinct was right: the chemist is often the first place illness shows up. The design was wrong. It treated the pharmacy as an informant on individual customers rather than as a sensor. This essay argues for the second idea. The tools that pharmaceutical companies use to track demand, read with the discipline of a marketing analyst and the caution of an epidemiologist, could become one of the cheapest early-warning systems India has.

Before India sees a doctor, it sees a counter

Nobody has an exact count of India’s pharmacies, which is a small irony for a sector that tracks every strip of paracetamol. The Competition Commission of India’s 2021 market study put the number of private retail chemists at about nine lakh. The trade body AIOCD speaks for about 12.4 lakh chemists and distributors together. The government told Parliament in 2023 that India has about 16 lakh registered pharmacists.

What matters more than the count is where these shops sit in people’s lives. A pooled analysis of Indian studies estimates that a little over half of people self-medicate, though individual studies vary widely. In one community survey in rural Hooghly, West Bengal, 48.5% of people with an acute illness treated themselves, and 59.3% of those got their medicine from a nearby shop. In interviews across all eleven districts of Delhi, people described the retail pharmacy as their first point of consultation for common ailments once home remedies had failed.

So when a fever spreads through a ward of Patna or a taluk in Kalaburagi, the first data point it creates is rarely a hospital record. It is a sale.

The ledger already sees illness

India’s medicine market is closely watched. Pharmarack valued the retail market at about ₹2.41 lakh crore in 2025. AIOCD-AWACS, Pharmarack and IQVIA publish monthly sales audits built from the books of stockists, the wholesalers who supply chemists, broken down by brand, pack and region. Brand managers use them to judge launches, plan supply and decide where to send medical representatives.

That ledger has already recorded India’s health history, if anyone cared to read it that way. Researchers who analysed IQVIA sales data found that COVID-19 likely added 216.4 million excess doses of adult antibiotics in India, including 38 million excess doses of azithromycin, concentrated between June and September 2020 as the first wave peaked. The country’s fever chart was written in paracetamol too: Dolo-650 sold about 350 crore tablets, worth ₹567 crore, between March 2020 and early 2022, according to IQVIA figures reported by Business Today.

The ledger also sees things the health system would rather not. A study in Lancet Regional Health – Southeast Asia used the same kind of stockist data to estimate that India consumed 5,071 million defined daily doses of antibiotics in 2019. Azithromycin was the most consumed molecule, and 47.1% of consumption was of formulations that had never been approved by the central drug regulator. A dataset that can see misuse at that scale can certainly see a spike in fever medicine in a district.

Marketing teams already know where fever is rising. They just call it a good month.

The world has tested the idea

The idea of reading drug sales as a health signal is not new, and it has been tested properly. In the United States, the University of Pittsburgh built a National Retail Data Monitor that, by 2003, received daily sales data from 10,000 stores, and from more than 15,000 by 2004. One study from the same Pittsburgh team found that sales of electrolyte products, used mostly for children, tracked hospital diagnoses of respiratory and diarrhoeal illness most closely when the sales were shifted about 1.7 weeks ahead of them. Outbreaks could be detected from sales 2.4 weeks earlier on average.

France’s Sentinelles network has worked with IQVIA (formerly IMS Health) since 1999, receiving dispensing data from about 14,000 pharmacies every week. A French study found that sales of non-prescription medicines detected gastroenteritis epidemics 2.25 weeks earlier, on average, than the doctors’ sentinel network. In December 2025, a study from Pinghu in China’s Zhejiang province found that sales of five categories of over-the-counter medicines tracked fever and respiratory cases at the local hospital, and still did so when sales led by one to two weeks.

The lesson is not that sales data is magic. It is that a few weeks of warning is realistic when the data is good and the analysis is careful. For a disease like dengue, two weeks is the difference between fogging a ward before the peak and counting beds after it.

India’s surveillance counts what walks into a government building

India has a serious surveillance system. The Integrated Disease Surveillance Programme began in 2004, with health workers, doctors and laboratories filing weekly reports on suspected, presumptive and confirmed cases. In April 2021 it moved to the Integrated Health Information Platform, which tracked 33 diseases at launch with near-real-time digital reporting, up from 18. The government now describes the programme as covering more than 40 diseases and syndromes.

Its blind spot is the private sector, where much of India first seeks care. Reporting is mandatory for public facilities and voluntary for private ones. A study of Maharashtra found that in 2012 only 50 private laboratories and 88 private hospitals had reported to the programme, against 1,762 public laboratories and 2,303 public facilities, and that only 18% of the private units reported consistently. We found no sign that retail pharmacies report to it at all.

Dengue shows what this costs. India recorded 289,235 dengue cases and 485 deaths in 2023, and 233,519 cases and 297 deaths in 2024. Those are the reported numbers. A study in the American Journal of Tropical Medicine and Hygiene estimated that, in 2006 to 2012, the true number of clinically diagnosed cases was about 282 times what was reported. Most people with dengue-like fever never become a statistic. Many of them do become a sale.

Tuberculosis shows it can work here

India has already turned the chemist into a reporting point for one disease, which shows both the potential and the friction. Since March 2014, Schedule H1 of the Drugs and Cosmetics Rules has required chemists to keep a separate register for an initial list of 46 drugs, including anti-TB medicines and newer antibiotics, recording the prescriber, the patient and the quantity, and to keep it for three years. In March 2018 the government made chemists who dispense anti-TB drugs responsible for notifying those patients, with penalties under the Indian Penal Code for failing to do so.

The pharmacies’ behaviour at the counter supports the idea that they can be partners. In a 2016 study in Delhi, Mumbai and Patna, researchers sent trained actors posing as patients to 622 pharmacies. When the actor described symptoms of TB, only 13% of interactions ended with a referral and no antibiotics or steroids. When the actor showed a confirmed TB diagnosis, 62% did, and not one pharmacy sold first-line TB drugs without a prescription. Chemists respond well when the signal is clear.

The sales data shows promise too. A study published in December 2025 compared cough syrup sales with TB notifications across all 33 districts of Rajasthan. Districts that sold more cough syrup had more private-sector TB notifications (a correlation of 0.63) and more missed notifications (0.39). The authors estimated that missed private-sector cases ran at about seven times the reported number. Next door, a programme in four districts of Pakistan’s Punjab province mapped where anti-TB drugs were being sold and enlisted 2,943 pharmacies to report. Notifications rose 34% over the baseline, against 8% in comparison districts.

Why it could go badly wrong

Every argument for sales surveillance should come with the story of Google Flu Trends. Google used search queries to estimate flu in the United States, and for a while it looked like the future. Then it overestimated flu in 100 of 108 weeks from August 2011, and at the peak of the 2012–13 season it predicted more than double the doctor visits that the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention recorded. The researchers who dissected the failure called it “big data hubris”: treating a large, convenient dataset as a substitute for measurement rather than a complement to it.

Medicine sales carry their own traps, and marketers know them better than anyone:

Promotions look like epidemics. In June 2015, New York City’s health department investigated a rise in antidiarrhoeal sales and concluded it probably reflected sales promotions, not illness.

Panic looks like disease. In Germany, purchases of pharmaceuticals jumped by more than 30% in March 2020 as people stockpiled paracetamol and cold medicine, then fell back.

Some signals simply do not work. In Japan, sales of over-the-counter cold medicines from about 1,100 pharmacies were not enough to predict influenza activity in advance.

Stock moves for commercial reasons. Stockist data shows medicines moving from wholesalers to shops, not from shops to patients. Our inference is that end-of-quarter stocking, price changes and new launches will move these numbers for reasons that have nothing to do with health.

Then there is privacy. India’s COVID-era orders collected names and phone numbers. A sentinel that frightens people away from the chemist would damage the very behaviour it depends on. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act defines personal data as data about an individual who can be identified by it. A weekly count of fever medicine sold in a pin code, with no names attached, falls outside that definition. The Rules were notified on 13 November 2025, and most of their obligations take effect in May 2027. That gives designers time to build the system the right way, around counts rather than customers.

How to build the sentinel

None of this needs new technology. A common assumption is that India’s GST system already captures every pharmacy sale. It does not: e-invoicing applies to business-to-business sales by firms with a turnover above ₹5 crore, so a chemist’s sale to a patient never reaches the portal. The data that exists sits with stockists, sales-audit firms, pharmacy software vendors and the large chains. The work is in assembling it and reading it well.

The second rule deserves emphasis because it is where marketing analytics earns its place. A demand planner forecasting next month’s paracetamol sales in eastern Uttar Pradesh typically models monsoon seasonality, festival weeks, distributor schemes and competitor launches. The gap between that forecast and what actually sells is, for the company, a forecasting error. For a district health officer, an unexplained positive gap in fever and ORS sales is a reason to send someone to look.

Who should care

Pharmaceutical companies hold the data and the analytical skills. Sharing aggregated, anonymised sales signals with public health authorities costs them little. It improves their own supply planning, because outbreaks are also stock-outs, and it is a more credible contribution to public health than another logo on a health camp banner.

Governments get a signal from the private sector without forcing a single private doctor to fill in a form. The platform to receive it, the Integrated Health Information Platform, already exists.

NGOs, CSR teams and donors get a better way to decide where to send help. Funding tends to follow familiar geographies and existing partners. A sales-based signal, checked against official data, can point a mobile clinic, a testing drive or a stock of ORS at the block where illness is rising this month, rather than the one where the office is.

The question for leaders

For pharmaceutical boards, the question is not whether their sales data has public value. It plainly does. The question is whether they will offer it, on terms that protect patients and their own commercial interests, before a regulator demands it on worse terms. For health officials, it is whether they will treat the chemist as a partner to be listened to rather than a suspect to be policed. India has nine lakh sentinels on its street corners already. They have been reporting for decades. So far, only the sales team has been listening.

What would change our view

If a prospective trial in Indian districts found that sales signals, once adjusted for promotions and stocking, gave no useful lead over the Integrated Health Information Platform, or raised false alarms often enough to waste field teams’ time, the case for building this system would be weak. The evidence so far is retrospective and comes from other countries. It needs testing here.
